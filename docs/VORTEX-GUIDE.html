00 — Start here

There is only one idea in this codebase

A cardboard box starts life as one flat sheet of board with cut lines and fold lines printed on it. That flat sheet is called a dieline. Everything Vortex does comes from a single stubborn rule: the dieline is the truth, and nothing is allowed to disagree with it.

Most 3D product configurators cheat. Someone draws a flat dieline for the printer, and separately someone models a nice-looking box in 3D software. Two files, two sources of truth. They drift. The 3D preview shows a box that is 2 mm taller than the one the factory will actually make, or the artwork lands 5 mm off the fold, and nobody notices until a pallet of misprinted cartons arrives.

Vortex refuses to have two files. The production dieline is imported once and becomes the geometric authority. The 2D editor draws on it. The 3D model is built from it. The folding motion pivots on its actual crease lines. The print-ready PDF is generated from it. There is no second shape anywhere in the system that could drift.

The north-star invariant. Stated in the repo as a literal equation, and enforced by automated gates:

fully flattened 3D structural geometry == canonical production dieline

Unfold the 3D box completely and you must get back the exact sheet you imported — not approximately, but to measured tolerance. On the reference carton the boundary difference is 4.5 × 10⁻¹³ mm.

Hold onto that one sentence and the rest of this document is just detail. Every engine below exists either to establish that invariant, to exploit it, or to prove it still holds.

01 — Motivation

What this actually solves

A customer wants to put their logo on a box and see what it will look like before paying for ten thousand of them. That sounds simple. It is not, because the box is a physical object with real dimensions and the printer needs a file that is correct to a fraction of a millimetre.

There are four hard problems stacked on top of each other:

Reading the factory's file
Real dielines arrive as PDF, SVG or DXF drawn by CAD software. Cut lines and fold lines are distinguished only by spot colour names — in the reference file, DieCutBlue means cut and DieCutRed means crease. Nothing labels them semantically. You have to know the convention and read it exactly.
Understanding the shape
A pile of line segments is not a box. Something has to work out that these particular lines enclose 17 separate panels, that this 8-sided hole is a window, and that these 16 crease chains are the hinges those panels rotate around.
Folding it correctly
Which panel is the floor? Which way does each flap fold? A carton that folds the wrong way is not a carton. Some of this is provable from geometry; some of it genuinely is not, and the system has to be honest about which is which.
Printing it correctly
The final file needs real bleed, real resolution, a colour profile the press understands, and technical layers the die-cutter reads. A screenshot of a 3D model is worthless here.

Where it bends. The third problem is the one that cannot be fully solved from a drawing. A flat sheet does not record its own board thickness, which flap tucks into which, or where the glue goes. Vortex's answer is not to guess — it is to refuse to guess, label every estimate as an estimate, and keep manufacturing certification as a separate gate that only a real converter can sign off.

02 — First principles

What a dieline is, precisely

Take a cereal box and carefully pull it apart at the glue. Flatten it. What you are holding is a dieline: one continuous piece of board, with knife cuts around the outside and pressed grooves where it folds.

In file form, a dieline is a set of vector paths carrying two meanings:

  • Cut — the knife goes all the way through. The outer boundary of the sheet, plus any holes such as a display window.
  • Crease — a blunt rule presses a groove so the board folds cleanly without cracking. It does not cut through.

The reference carton this repository is built around is a lock-bottom carton with a window, nominally 300 × 150 × 200 mm. Its flat sheet measures 742.4 × 500.003 mm and decomposes into 17 panels joined by 16 crease chains.

Schematic only — proportions simplified. The real sheet has chamfered flap corners and an 8-edge window. Solid = cut, dashed = crease.

Why the middle row matters

The five panels across the middle are the body band. Fold them at the four vertical creases and they close into a rectangular tube — the walls of the box. The narrow panel at the far left is the seam, the flap that glues to the opposite wall to hold the tube shut. The flaps above and below become the top and the lock-bottom.

Two of the body panels are broad 200 mm and two are narrow 150 mm, giving a 200 × 150 mm cross-section, and the band is 300 mm tall. That is exactly the product's nominal 300 × 150 × 200 mm — and the engine verifies the assembled model closes to those numbers rather than trusting the filename.

03 — The sequence

From file to folding box, in seven stages

This is the one part of the system that genuinely is a sequence: each stage can only run because the one before it succeeded. Everything else in this manual is reference material you can read in any order.

01

Import — read the file exactly

A PDF, SVG or DXF is parsed into a canonical dieline: paths in real millimetres, each tagged cut or crease by its spot-colour name, each keeping a record of exactly which source entity it came from.

src/lib/structure/import-pdf-raw.ts · import-svg.ts · import-dxf.ts

02

Topology — turn lines into a graph

Line segments become a planar graph of vertices and edges. Endpoints that nearly meet are snapped — but only within a hard 0.02 mm budget, and every repair is recorded. On the reference file exactly four repairs occur, the largest 0.0147 mm.

src/lib/structure/topology.ts

03

Panels — find the enclosed faces

Walking that graph finds the closed regions: 17 panels, one of which owns an 8-edge window as a genuine hole rather than a painted-on rectangle.

src/lib/structure/topology.ts → extractStructuralPanels

04

Meshes — give the panels thickness

Each panel is triangulated into a solid slab with a printed outer face, an unprinted inner face and a visible board edge, still expressed in sheet millimetres. Holes stay holes.

src/lib/structure/structural-mesh.ts

05

Rig — build the hinge hierarchy

One panel is chosen as the root. Every other panel is attached to a parent through a hinge whose axis is a real source crease line. The result is a tree: 17 panels, 16 hinges, no cycles, everything reachable.

src/lib/structure/structural-rig.ts · structural-tree.ts

06

Fold — drive it with absolute angles

Folding writes an absolute target angle onto every hinge. Zero degrees is an exact identity matrix, so the flat pose restores the original sheet coordinates perfectly, however many times you fold.

src/lib/configurator/unfold-plan.ts · hinge-animation.ts

07

Output — print and manufacture

The same canonical geometry produces the production PDF and the manufacturing SVG, with the cut and crease linework as real technical separations.

src/lib/print/ · src/server/production/

Fail closed. Every stage refuses to continue on bad input rather than producing a plausible-looking wrong answer. An unclassified stroke, a non-manifold boundary or a hinge that does not resolve to a real source crease is an error, not a warning.

The engines, and what each one owns

"Engine" here means a self-contained body of logic with one responsibility and a clear boundary. Six of them, plus a platform that stores and serves everything.

How they layer

Dependencies run one way. The structural engine knows nothing about React, the database or products — it is pure geometry. The configurator depends on structure. The UI depends on the configurator. The platform depends on none of them for its own correctness.

Import direction is a rule, not a habit. src/lib/structure must never import from src/lib/configurator. The reverse is fine. This keeps the geometry engine testable in isolation and prevents a dependency cycle — it is why the shared fold-capture module lives on the configurator side.

05 — Core

The structural engine, layer by layer

This is the part that reads the factory's drawing and works out what shape it describes. If you only read one section, read this one — 28 files and roughly ten thousand lines, all of it in service of not guessing.

The vector domain

Everything starts with a shared vocabulary. A CanonicalDieline is millimetres, x-right-y-down, with a source record and an explicit tolerance set. Nothing downstream is allowed to invent its own units or its own idea of "close enough".

ToleranceValueGoverns
coordinateEpsilonMm1e-9Floating-point equality only — never geometric repair
topologySnapMm0.01Maximum endpoint repair distance
curveFlatteningMm0.05Curve-to-polyline chord deviation
boundaryComparisonMm0.05Accepted source-vs-derived boundary deviation
metricSampleSpacingMm0.05Spacing between measurement samples

Import: three formats, one rule

SVG, DXF and PDF each get a dedicated importer — and the PDF path has two variants. The ordinary one reads a normal vector PDF. The raw authority one reads the page's content stream directly to recover named spot-colour separations, because that is the only place the cut/crease distinction actually lives.

On the reference file the importer sees DieCutBlue painted 78 times (cut), DieCutRed 24 times (crease), and DieCutGreen once — a presentation band that is explicitly ignored rather than silently treated as geometry.

No inference. A stroke with no explicit structural classification is an error. The importer will not decide that a thin blue line is "probably" a cut. If the file does not say, the import fails.

Topology: from soup to structure

CAD files are rarely perfectly closed. Lines that should meet miss by microns. topology.ts splits paths at intersections, welds near-coincident endpoints, and builds a planar graph — then walks it to find enclosed faces.

The repair budget is deliberately tiny and fully audited. The reference profile records exactly four endpoint-to-span repairs with a maximum of 0.0147 mm, and the manifest carries an explicit warning that roughly 0.3 mm source details are real evidence and must not be smoothed away.

Meshes and the three faces

Each panel becomes a slab. The geometry carries three material groups in a fixed order, and that order is load-bearing — group 0 is what the customer's artwork lands on: 

group 0  printed outside   (top face,   normal +Y)
group 1  inner board       (bottom face, normal -Y)
group 2  exposed cut edge  (the board's visible thickness)

The rig: a tree, not a pile

A folding carton is a kinematic tree. One root panel stays put; every other panel hangs off a parent through exactly one hinge. The rig validates this hard: 16 hinges for 17 panels, no panel with two parents, no cycles, every panel reachable from the root, and every hinge resolving to real source crease addresses.

The certified hinge hierarchy. Twelve flap hinges hang off the four body panels; the body chain itself is geometry-proven.

The quality gates

structural-acceptance.ts runs twelve source-locked gates against the real file. All twelve must pass or the pipeline stops:

06 — Motion

How the box actually folds

Animating a folding box is deceptively hard. The naive approach — nudge each hinge a bit every frame — drifts. Fold and unfold a hundred times and the box no longer closes. Vortex solves this by never using relative motion at all.

Absolute angles, always

Every hinge has an absolute target angle. The runtime writes that angle onto the hinge matrix directly. It never adds a delta to the current rotation. Zero degrees writes an exact identity matrix, so the flat pose is bit-for-bit the original sheet, not an approximation that accumulates error.

This single decision gives three properties for free: rapid clicking cannot corrupt the pose, Forward and Backward traverse the same states in opposite directions, and a hundred fold cycles leave zero drift.

Four phases, assembled to flat

The fold is authored as four ordered phases. Reading them backwards gives you assembly; forwards gives you flattening.

#PhaseMoves
1final-closureThe last visible flap that closes the box
2major-closureThe main top flaps
3secondary-flapsDust flaps and the bottom lock
4bodyThe four body-tube hinges — the walls lay flat last

Timing that came from measurement

The motion envelope is not a designer's guess. It was measured from reference material and is enforced by the compiler — a phase outside these bounds throws rather than rendering: 

duration   450–700 ms per hinge
stagger     50–150 ms between hinges in a phase
easing     easeInOutCubic   (no spring, no bounce, no overshoot)

Camera independence. Fold logic never touches camera or orbit state. A product may declare a fixed presentation rotation, but that lives outside the hinge tree entirely. This is checked by a dedicated test — tests/unfold/camera-independence.test.ts.

The 100-cycle certificate

structural-runtime-quality.ts runs the model through a hundred assembled-to-flat cycles and asserts that geometry was never rebuilt, every matrix stayed finite, hinge matrices returned to exact identity at flat, and the flat world pose did not drift. On the reference carton both drift measurements come back at exactly zero.

07 — Mapping

Artwork, and the mirror that hid for months

Getting a logo onto a 3D box means deciding, for every point on the model, which pixel of the flat artwork belongs there. Get the direction wrong and every logo comes out backwards — while the geometry, the normals and the tests all still look perfectly fine.

Global sheet coordinates

Because panels stay in canonical sheet coordinates, texture mapping is almost trivial: a point's texture coordinate is just its position on the sheet, normalised. Artwork therefore flows continuously across creases by construction — there is no per-panel atlas to misalign. 

u = x / sheetWidth
v = 1 - y / sheetHeight     // sheet y runs down, texture v runs up

The bug that was invisible

For a long time u was written as 1 - x / width. That inversion is genuinely correct for the legacy carton builder, which places each panel at its final position on the assembled box — there, the physical act of flipping the printed sheet over before folding is already baked into the geometry, and the texture has to compensate.

Structural panels are different: they never move from their sheet position. Applying the same inversion flipped the sheet a second time, mirroring artwork on every assembled panel. Worse, the UV round-trip gate inverted u exactly the same way, so it was self-consistent and completely blind to the defect.

How it was caught. Not by reasoning — by measuring. The assembled rig was walked panel by panel, comparing the winding of each printed triangle in texture space against its winding as seen from outside the box. All 17 panels came back mirrored. The fix is locked by tests/structure/structural-chirality.test.ts, which was confirmed to fail against the old behaviour before being accepted.

Handedness: which way the tube closes

A related question has the same flavour. The body tube can close two ways — the walls can rotate toward positive or negative depth. Both produce a geometrically valid rectangular tube, so geometry alone cannot choose.

Measurement can. Only one option puts the printed face on the outside of the carton, and the difference is dramatic:

HandednessAssembled sizePrinted face outside
negative-depth350 × 320 × 300 mm12 of 17
positive-depth200.0 × 150.0 × 300.0 mm17 of 17

Positive-depth closes to exactly the nominal carton with every panel printed-side-out. Negative-depth does not even close the box — the flaps splay outward. The decision is now recorded with that measurement as its stated evidence.

08 — Output

The print engine

The file that goes to the factory is not a picture of the 3D model. It is a physical-size document generated from the same geometry, with the artwork placed at real resolution and the cut and crease lines carried as technical layers the die-cutter reads.

Printer profiles

Each product references a reusable PrinterProfile that controls the PDF standard and output condition, render resolution, minimum acceptable image PPI, a raster-memory ceiling, and the exact naming, spot colours, line weights and overprint behaviour of the cut and crease separations.

Two ship today. pdfx4-srgb-packaging-v1 is a generic colour-managed PDF/X-4 handoff embedding the official sRGB2014 ICC profile. vortex-carton-works-coated-offset-v1 is a simulated converter contract that proves the reusable path — 3 mm bleed, 300 PPI rendering, 250 PPI minimum placed-image resolution, overprinting CutContour and Crease separations.

Preflight blocks bad jobs

preflightPrintJob refuses to export when it finds:

  • a design belonging to a different product
  • invalid or missing physical dimensions
  • a dieline with no cut paths
  • an image whose true pixel dimensions are unknown
  • an image below the profile's minimum effective PPI
  • a render job that would exceed the raster-memory limit

Honest about what it is not. A generic colour-managed file is not a substitute for a converter's press-specific approval. The simulated profile stays explicitly marked simulated-company until a real converter signs off a physical proof.

09 — Materials

The embroidery engine

For fabric products — a t-shirt, say — a flat printed logo looks wrong. Real embroidery has thread direction, height and sheen. This engine fakes all three convincingly, entirely in the browser.

The chain is image → stitches → material maps:

  1. Preprocess — decode and bound the artwork.
  2. Quantize — reduce to a small thread palette, since real embroidery uses a limited number of thread colours.
  3. Distance transform — an exact Euclidean distance field (edt.ts) derives shape interiors and stitch direction.
  4. Plan stitches — lay out a stitch field with direction and density.
  5. Render maps — emit normal and roughness maps so light catches the thread the way it would on real cloth.

Resolution is tiered: 2.8 px/mm for interactive preview, 8 px/mm for full quality — roughly three pixels across a 0.4 mm thread, the point at which strands stop aliasing into mush. Heavy work runs in a Web Worker so the interface never stalls.

Privacy by construction. The customer's image is read and never written, and never leaves the device. The module knows nothing about React, three.js, or which surface the result lands on.

10 — Platform

Storing, serving and protecting the work

The engines above are pure computation. The platform is everything that remembers: who you are, what you designed, which version of the product you designed it against, and what exactly was sent to the printer.

The central contract

A DesignDocument is the only source of truth for a customer's design. The 2D editor mutates it; the 3D preview, thumbnails, bulk personalisation and production output all derive from it. There is no parallel render model.

Immutability everywhere

RuleWhy it matters
Published product and template versions never change in placeA design made last year still resolves against the product as it was then
Every save creates a new revision via compare-and-swapTwo tabs cannot silently overwrite each other
Production binds project + revision + product version + configurationAn export is reproducible; it names exactly what it was made from
All authoritative inputs are re-resolved server-sideThe browser cannot talk the server into a different price or size

Identity, including guests

A customer is either an authenticated user (Better Auth session) or a signed guest carried in an HMAC-signed, HttpOnly, SameSite=Lax cookie. Both resolve to a ProjectOwner. When a guest signs up, their work is claimed atomically: both identities must arrive on the same request, it is same-origin and rate-limit protected, it goes through a claim ledger, and the guest cookie is cleared on success.

Knowing a UUID is not authorisation. Owner-scoped reads always include the owner type and ID. Guessing a project id gets you nothing.

Persistence is SQLite at schema v16, defaulting to .data/vortex.sqlite. There are 47 API route handlers under /api/v1. Public DTOs never contain storage keys, filesystem paths, provider internals or credentials.

Deployment limits, stated plainly. The rate limiter and job runners are in-process. Horizontal deployment needs a shared rate limiter and an external worker. VORTEX_DATABASE=postgresql fails closed until the PostgreSQL repositories are complete — the schema is documented, but SQLite is the only runnable adapter today.

11 — Ingestion

Turning any 3D model into a product

Not every product is a folding carton. A mug, a camera, a t-shirt arrives as an ordinary 3D model with no idea which part of it is printable. This Python pipeline works that out.

12 — Proof

The golden reference, and what certified means

Claiming the engine works is easy. The golden reference is how the claim gets tested: one real, licensed production dieline from a real converter, locked by checksum, that the whole pipeline must reproduce.

The reference is a lock-bottom carton with a window, identified by SHA-256 b6b8cda5…f557. The file itself is not in the repository — it is licensed material. What is committed is a manifest recording its hash, page boxes, separation names, paint counts and expected geometry, so the engine can verify it is looking at exactly the approved file.

Two lanes, deliberately separate

Reference recreation certified
Does the software faithfully reproduce what the reference shows? This is answerable from the file plus visual evidence.
Manufacturing construction blocked by design
Is this how the carton is actually built in a factory? This needs converter data the drawing does not contain, and no amount of visual checking can substitute.

The gates

Certification requires six fixed-camera captures — flat 2D, flat 3D, mid-fold, body erect, secondary flaps, and a major/final closure pair — plus ten hard gates and a score of at least 45 out of 50.

CategoryScoreBasis
Geometry alignment10 / 10Flat 3D equals source to 4.5e-13 mm; body closes to exactly 200.0 × 150.0 × 300.0 mm
Mapping continuity10 / 10Correct chirality, printed face exterior on all 17 panels, no crease jumps
Fold-pose match8 / 10Diagonal lock creases held flat — the reference does not recover their signed angles
Motion match7 / 8Verified against the distilled envelope, not frame-by-frame against the raw recording
CAD visual quality6 / 6Exact polygons, real board depth, true window void
Material / lighting4 / 6Deterministic harness lighting; no image-based lighting or contact shadows
Total45 / 50Threshold is 45

The finalizer emits exactly one verdict string, and it is careful:

REFERENCE_RECREATION_CERTIFIED_NOT_MANUFACTURING_CERTIFICATION

Both deductions are evidence limits, not implementation defects. The original reference screenshots are recorded by hash in the fixture manifest but marked untracked and not redistributed, so motion could only be checked against the distilled envelope. The score sits exactly at the threshold — worth knowing rather than glossing over.

What executing this lane actually found

The certification path had never been run end-to-end against the real file. Doing so surfaced three defects that every synthetic test fixture had hidden:

  1. The boundary comparison reduced ~123,000 samples with Math.max(...array), overflowing the JavaScript engine's argument stack at around 111,000 — so both verifiers crashed on any real production-sized dieline.
  2. The UV inversion described in §07, mirroring artwork on every assembled panel.
  3. The handedness choice, putting the printed face inside the box and leaving the flaps splayed open.

All three are now regression-locked, and each new test was confirmed to fail against the old behaviour before being accepted — a test that cannot fail proves nothing.

17 — Vocabulary

Glossary

Dieline
The flat plan of a package: cut lines, fold lines, and the panels between them.
Panel
One enclosed region of the sheet — a wall, a flap, a tab.
Crease
A pressed groove that lets board fold cleanly without cracking. Not a cut.
Hinge
The software counterpart of a crease: a rotation axis joining a parent panel to a child.
Body band / tube
The row of panels that closes into the four walls of the carton.
Seam
The narrow flap glued to the opposite wall to hold the tube shut.
Lock bottom
A base that interlocks as it folds, so it holds without glue.
Spot colour / separation
A named ink channel. Here they carry meaning: DieCutBlue = cut, DieCutRed = crease.
Chirality
Handedness — whether a shape or a mapping is mirrored. The reason logos come out backwards.
UV
A texture coordinate: which pixel of the flat artwork belongs at this point on the 3D surface.
Bleed
Artwork extended past the trim line so a slight cutting error leaves no white edge.
PPI
Pixels per inch at final printed size — the real measure of whether an image is sharp enough.
PDF/X-4
A print-exchange PDF standard with strict colour-management rules.
ICC profile
A colour description telling the press how to reproduce the file's colours.
GLB
A single-file 3D model format used for non-carton products.
Manifold
A surface that closes properly, with every edge used by the expected number of faces.
Fail closed
On uncertainty, stop with an error rather than continue with a guess.
